Movement 1 of 4

Opening movement of a forty-minute pitch

I would like to begin with a warning.

If the idea I am about to present is wrong, then it should be possible to reject it on its merits.

But if it is right, then the first obstacle to understanding it will not be its complexity.

The first obstacle will be what you already think you know.

I do not mean that as an insult to your intelligence or your expertise. I mean almost the opposite.

Whenever we encounter a new idea, understanding begins by asking what the idea resembles. We compare it with concepts we already possess. We place it within an existing field. We ask whether it belongs to philosophy, linguistics, psychology, neuroscience, information theory, semantics, or logic.

Normally, this is indispensable. Recognition allows us to orient ourselves.

But recognition can also occur too early.

A new phenomenon may be assimilated to a familiar explanation before we have properly seen what requires explanation.

So for the next forty minutes, I am not asking you to suspend disbelief.

I am asking you to delay recognition.

When you find yourself thinking, “This sounds like something I already know,” pause for a moment.

Do not yet ask:

“What established theory does this belong to?”

Ask instead:

“What phenomenon is being pointed at?”

That distinction is important because the proposal I am making does not begin with a new definition of language, meaning, thought, consciousness, or representation.

It begins with an event that happens constantly and that ordinary fluency makes almost impossible to notice.

A sentence becomes complete.

That sounds trivial.

Of course sentences become complete. People finish speaking. Punctuation marks the end of a written expression. Grammars distinguish complete sentences from fragments. Listeners recognise when someone has finished making a point.

But none of those observations identifies the event I mean.

I am not referring merely to the moment when sound stops.

A speaker may stop halfway through a sentence.

I am not referring merely to grammatical well-formedness.

A sentence may be perfectly grammatical and still fail to become intelligible.

I am not referring merely to the successful transmission of information.

A question, a command, a refusal, a joke, a confession, a promise and a line of poetry do not all communicate information in the same way.

And I am not referring simply to understanding what the words mean.

We can understand every individual word in an expression and still wait for the expression to become complete as an act.

Something occurs when intelligibility lands.

Something becomes sufficiently determinate that it can now be accepted, rejected, remembered, repeated, questioned, denied, translated, quoted, obeyed, resisted or inherited by a later sentence.

My proposal begins there.

It begins not with what a sentence represents, but with how anything becomes complete enough to stand as a representation at all.

Representation as a starting point

A great deal of modern thought begins with representation.

The mind represents the world.

Perception gives us representations of objects.

Memory retains representations of past events.

Language represents thoughts.

Symbols represent things.

Models represent systems.

Images represent scenes.

Scientific theories represent structures or processes that may not be directly observable.

There are many disagreements about what representations are, where they exist, how they acquire content, whether they resemble what they represent, and whether cognition can be adequately explained in representational terms.

There are also important philosophical traditions that resist the idea that representation should be treated as fundamental.

So I am not claiming that all contemporary philosophy or cognitive science rests upon one simple representational doctrine.

My point is narrower.

Whether representation is defended, revised or rejected, it remains one of the central terms through which we describe cognition.

And representation usually enters the discussion as something already capable of standing for something else.

A neural pattern represents an object.

A word represents a concept.

A sentence represents a state of affairs.

A mental image represents a scene.

Once we possess such an item, we can ask how it is encoded, stored, manipulated, retrieved or communicated.

But there is a prior question.

Before something can stand for something else, how does it become sufficiently complete to stand at all?

What gives it the unity required to count as one thing?

What distinguishes it from what it is not?

What allows it to remain available after the act in which it arose has ended?

What makes it capable of entering another act as something already given?

Representation presupposes an achievement.

Something has become determinate enough to bear.

The representational account may tell us what an item stands for. It may tell us how that item is used by a cognitive system. But it does not necessarily tell us how the item first acquired the completed intelligibility that permits it to function as a term in subsequent thought.

My inquiry begins with that completion.

The simplest formulation I can offer is this:

Representation asks:

“What does this stand for?”

I am asking:

“How did this become something capable of standing?”

That may initially sound like a highly abstract question.

It is not.

We can watch the phenomenon happen in ordinary speech.

“Draw a circle”

Suppose I say to you:

“Draw a circle.”

Before I speak, there may be no issue concerning circles. Nothing in the environment may call for one. You may not have been thinking about drawing. There may be no practical deficiency waiting to be repaired.

Then the sentence lands.

“Draw a circle.”

You can comply.

You can refuse.

You can ask why.

You can say that you do not understand.

You can ignore me behaviourally.

But you cannot make it the case that the sentence did not enter the situation.

Even a refusal inherits what the sentence has established.

“No, I will not draw a circle.”

Your refusal is not independent of the command. It re-enters the condition created by it. It opposes what has already become available as a possible act.

The command did not simply answer an unsettled situation.

It introduced an unsettledness that had not previously existed in that form.

It created a demand, and with that demand it created a field of possible responses: compliance, refusal, delay, evasion, negotiation, confusion.

This means that the unsettled condition to which a sentence relates is not always historically prior to the sentence.

Sometimes an unsettled condition already exists and a sentence answers it.

“What time does the meeting begin?”

“Nine o’clock.”

The uncertainty precedes the answer.

But sometimes the sentence intervenes in a relatively settled environment and institutes the condition under which it will matter.

“I resign.”

“You are under arrest.”

“I forgive you.”

“The building is on fire.”

Each sentence changes what can now intelligibly follow.

The sentence does not merely enter an existing field of relevance. It may establish the field.

It may make the world ask.

The unsolicited confession

Consider a more severe example.

Someone says:

“I killed a person last night.”

Imagine that nothing in the preceding conversation called for such a statement.

No crime was being discussed. No question was being asked. No confession was expected.

The sentence is radically unsolicited.

Yet its lack of contextual necessity does not make it inconsequential.

Once spoken, it creates a field of unsettledness.

Is the statement true?

Is the speaker dangerous?

Who was killed?

What must the listener do?

Should the police be contacted?

Is this a confession, a threat, a delusion, a joke or a quotation?

The sentence simultaneously determines and destabilises.

It determines something within the world asserted by the speaker: the speaker claims responsibility for a killing at a specified time.

But by establishing that determination, it unsettles the wider situation.

This matters because we are often tempted to describe a sentence as though it exists in order to satisfy a need that was already present.

A question requires an answer.

An uncertainty requires clarification.

A disorder requires correction.

An incomplete thought requires completion.

Sometimes that is true.

But sometimes the sentence creates its own need.

Sometimes it introduces a distinction, a demand or a possibility that did not govern the situation before it was spoken.

And once the sentence has landed, the mind can reconstruct the situation as though the sentence had always been waiting to be said.

Its arrival can produce the appearance of its own necessity.

That is not an incidental feature of language.

It is central to command, accusation, revelation, rhetoric, political speech, advertising, narrative and public discourse.

Language does not merely fill openings.

It creates openings.

It does not merely answer questions.

It determines what the question will be.

News and public discourse

Now consider the news.

Imagine that you are listening to commentary about an issue in which you have no immediate practical interest.

It may concern an election in another country, a financial regulation, a military dispute, a court case, an institutional scandal or a change in monetary policy.

You have no direct responsibility for the outcome.

You may never act upon the information.

You may not even regard the issue as especially important.

Nevertheless, completed acts of intelligibility are landing.

A person is introduced as credible or untrustworthy.

An event is described as a crisis, an opportunity, a scandal, a correction or an inevitability.

A causal relation is established.

A motive is attributed.

A distinction is drawn between responsible and irresponsible positions.

A prediction is made.

An analogy is introduced.

A field of possible judgment is being constructed.

The absence of immediate behavioural consequence does not mean that nothing has happened.

The report may leave behind something available for later thought.

A name becomes associated with danger.

A country becomes associated with instability.

A policy becomes associated with decline.

A movement becomes associated with extremism.

A public figure becomes associated with competence.

Weeks or months later, when the subject reappears, you do not encounter it from an entirely neutral beginning.

The later statement enters a field already structured by earlier landings.

This process is cumulative.

One sentence completes.

What it leaves behind enters the next sentence.

Commentary relates events.

Punditry assigns motives.

Headlines establish salience.

Repetition increases familiarity.

Expert status lends authority.

Eventually, a sequence of completed utterances presents itself not as one possible organisation of events, but as the world simply given.

This does not mean audiences are passive.

People resist.

They reinterpret.

They compare sources.

They forget.

They reject conclusions.

But resistance itself frequently occurs after landing.

Suppose a commentator says:

“This policy will destroy the economy.”

You may reject the statement immediately.

You may regard it as absurd, partisan or unsupported.

But to reject it, you must first receive the projected relation between the policy and economic destruction.

The rejection may prevent that relation from stabilising as belief.

It does not mean the relation was never introduced as an intelligible possibility.

This suggests a distinction between two forms of consequence.

The first is behavioural consequence.

What did the listener do?

Did they vote differently?

Did they buy something?

Did they contact a representative?

Did they change their behaviour?

The second is what I will call, provisionally, resolutive consequence.

How was the field available to later thought altered by what had landed?

What became familiar?

What became salient?

What became plausible?

Which distinctions became easier to re-enter?

Which explanations became available without being rebuilt?

A sentence can have little observable behavioural effect and still have substantial resolutive consequence.

It can prepare the conditions under which a later sentence will be understood.

The production of the question

Public discourse therefore does not merely provide answers to independently existing public questions.

It helps decide what the questions are.

A presenter says:

“The real question is whether the government has lost control.”

But perhaps that was not previously the real question.

The sentence institutes a field.

Guests are now invited to answer it.

Evidence is selected in relation to it.

Events are organised around it.

A refusal to answer may itself be interpreted as evidence.

The sentence participates in producing the unsettled condition to which the remainder of the discussion is then presented as a response.

And because the sentence is fluent, authoritative and contextually supported, the field it establishes may appear to have existed before the sentence.

The discourse can conceal its own constructive action.

It appears merely to describe what is already there.

This is why representation cannot be the entire story.

The commentator is not always simply representing a completed public object called “the crisis,” “the loss of control,” “the threat,” or “the recovery.”

The sentence may participate in completing that object as something the public can subsequently represent.

Later discourse then inherits it.

“How severe is the crisis?”

“Who caused the crisis?”

“When will the crisis end?”

The object now functions as an available term.

But its availability required an earlier completion.

Something had to land.

The first claim

I can now state the first major claim of this presentation.

A completed sentence does not merely carry representational content into a pre-existing field of thought.

It may participate in creating the field within which its content can subsequently function as a representation.

This means that representation may not be the primitive unit we often take it to be.

Before a thought, symbol, phrase or sentence can represent, something must become sufficiently determinate to survive the act in which it arises.

It must become capable of re-entry.

It must be available for acceptance, denial, memory, repetition, quotation, translation or inheritance.

I use the word “survive” carefully.

I do not mean that the original mental or physical event continues to occur.

When a person speaks, the sound disappears.

The neural activity changes.

The bodily act ends.

Yet something accomplished by the utterance may remain available.

The event ceases.

Its bearing does not necessarily cease.

This gives us a second question:

How can a completed act stop occurring without ceasing to matter?

Dickens

Consider one sentence:

“It was the best of times, it was the worst of times.”

Charles Dickens wrote those words in the nineteenth century.

Now imagine a man sitting in a conservatory in 2026, reading A Tale of Two Cities.

His partner enters and asks:

“What are you reading?”

He reads the sentence aloud:

“It was the best of times, it was the worst of times.”

She replies:

“Oh, Dickens.”

What exactly has happened?

The original physical act of writing is gone.

The original mental event is gone.

The nineteenth-century material circumstances are gone.

The sentence has passed through editions, printers, paper, typography, libraries, publishers and readers.

It has now become visual perception in one person, speech from that person, auditory perception in another, and recognition:

“Oh, Dickens.”

What remained available across that history?

It cannot be the original sound, because there may have been no original sound.

It cannot be the original marks, because the present copy contains different physical marks.

It cannot be Dickens’s original neural state.

It cannot simply be the book as a material object, because innumerable materially distinct copies carry what we recognise as the same sentence.

We might say that the meaning was transmitted.

But that names the phenomenon without yet explaining its structure.

What made the sentence capable of surviving through materially different carriers?

What allowed the partner to recognise not merely English words, but a completed literary act with an identifiable source?

The original act did not literally travel through time.

Dickens’s act ended.

What the act accomplished became available through a carrier.

The reader did not reproduce Dickens’s original consciousness.

He re-realised an intelligible completion under different material and historical conditions.

His partner then inherited that completion through another carrier: his voice.

The continuity is therefore not the continuous existence of the original event.

It is the recoverability of what the event completed.

A completed act can cease occurring without ceasing to matter.

That, in its simplest form, is the phenomenon I want to investigate.

Survivor and carrier

We now need two preliminary distinctions.

The first is between a survivor and a carrier.

A carrier is the material or embodied condition through which something becomes available again.

Ink on paper can be a carrier.

Sound can be a carrier.

Digital text can be a carrier.

Memory can function as a carrier.

Gesture can be a carrier.

A carrier is necessary for re-entry into a new act, but the carrier is not identical with what is carried.

The same sentence may appear in different fonts, voices, languages and media.

The carrier changes.

Something remains recognisable.

I call what remains available the survivor of the completed act.

The term does not yet commit us to a particular metaphysics.

It does not tell us whether the survivor is semantic, logical, formal, social, neural or some combination of these.

It simply marks the explanatory requirement.

Something has completed in such a way that it can bear upon acts that occur after the originating event has ended.

The survivor can be inherited.

It can be re-entered.

It can be transformed.

It can be opposed.

It can be combined with other survivors.

It can remain dormant and later become active.

It can enter a mind that has no knowledge of the conditions under which it was first established.

That is what happens constantly in language.

Reading as re-realisation

Reading, on this account, is not the passive reception of an intact object called meaning.

Nor is it unrestricted invention by the reader.

It is constrained re-realisation.

The reader performs a new act.

That act is materially and historically distinct from the author’s act.

But it is constrained by what the earlier act made available through the carrier.

This allows us to avoid two extremes.

At one extreme, meaning is imagined as a self-contained object transported unchanged from one mind to another.

At the other, meaning is imagined as being freely constructed afresh by each interpreter.

The phenomenon suggests something more structured.

The original act is not present.

The later act is genuinely new.

Yet the later act is not arbitrary.

Something absent remains authoritative without remaining continuously present.

That may be one of the deepest facts concealed by ordinary language.

The absent can govern the present.

A sentence written by a dead author can constrain what counts as an accurate quotation.

A mathematical proof can constrain a student centuries later.

A legal judgment can continue to bear upon cases never imagined by the judge.

A promise can govern future conduct after the sound of the promise has vanished.

A scientific result can enter experiments performed by researchers who never encountered the original investigators.

Language, law, mathematics, science and culture depend upon completed acts remaining recoverable after their occurrence has ended.

And yet we rarely begin our theories there.

We begin with information.

We begin with representation.

We begin with symbols.

We begin with mental content.

We begin with communication.

My proposal is that these all presuppose something more elementary:

the completion of intelligibility.

What I am not yet claiming

At this point, I want to be precise about what has and has not been claimed.

I have not yet claimed that I possess a complete scientific theory of cognition.

I have not claimed that existing linguistics is mistaken.

I have not claimed that representation is unreal.

I have not claimed that every act of understanding is conscious.

I have not claimed that every sentence succeeds.

And I have not yet asked you to accept any classification system.

I am asking only whether the following phenomenon is real:

An intelligible act becomes complete.

The originating event ends.

Something accomplished by that event remains available.

That survivor enters later acts and conditions how they become intelligible.

If that phenomenon does not exist, the remainder of my proposal fails.

But if it does exist, then we require an account of its structure.

We need to ask:

What exactly is completed?

How does completion differ from mere termination?

What survives?

How does a survivor enter a later act?

How can one completed act condition another?

And are there recurrent structural forms through which completed intelligibility lands?

It is at this point—not before—that the architecture I am proposing becomes relevant.

My claim is that completed intelligibility is not shapeless.

A sentence does not simply end and leave behind an undifferentiated quantity called meaning.

It performs work.

It moves with an orientation.

And something survives the movement.

If that is correct, then the completion of a sentence may be describable through a finite architecture.

That is the proposal I will now begin to show.

Movement 2 of 4

Second movement: The architecture of landing

I have argued that something happens when a sentence becomes complete.

Attention is no longer left entirely open.

Some continuations remain possible.

Others have been removed.

A relation stands.

A distinction holds.

A condition is established.

Something has landed.

The next question is whether every landing is merely unique, or whether completed intelligibility has recurring forms.

My proposal is that it does.

Not recurring meanings.

Not recurring topics.

Not recurring grammatical constructions.

Recurring forms of completion.

That distinction is essential.

Two sentences may concern entirely different subjects and nevertheless complete through the same structural movement.

Two sentences may express nearly the same proposition and nevertheless land differently.

So the object of inquiry is not primarily what the sentence is about.

It is how the sentence brings attention to rest.

A finite completion from an open field

At any moment, attention is capable of moving in many directions.

A person can notice one feature rather than another.

A speaker can continue a sentence in multiple ways.

An event can be described under different distinctions.

A relation can be foregrounded, ignored, corrected, expanded, denied or integrated into a larger whole.

The field is not literally infinite in every measurable sense.

The phrase “infinite field of possible attention” names the practical openness of what may still be selected, related or continued.

A serious sentence contracts that openness.

It does not contain the whole of reality.

It does not exhaust everything that could be said.

It makes one lawful selection from more possibility than it can include.

That is why a sentence can be exact without being exhaustive.

Its completion consists not in containing everything, but in making something stand.

The sentence is therefore not best understood as a container filled with meaning.

It is an event of lawful contraction.

Attention moves through possibility.

Incompatible continuations are progressively removed.

A particular completion becomes available.

Meaning, in this limited structural sense, is what remains capable of standing once incompatible continuations have been excluded.

This is not yet a complete theory of meaning.

It does not replace reference, implication, context, association, intention or interpretation.

It identifies the form of the landing, not the whole semantic life of the sentence.

That limit must remain explicit.

Otherwise, a theory of completion becomes a theory that claims to explain everything and therefore ceases to discriminate anything.

Watch the movement

Consider the sentence:

“The door is closed.”

What has happened?

A condition has been established.

The door is presented as standing in a settled state.

Now compare it with:

“The door closed.”

The subject matter is nearly identical.

The same door may be involved.

The same physical condition may obtain at the end.

But the listener is not made to stand in the same relation to it.

“The door is closed” places attention within a condition.

“The door closed” makes attention traverse an occurrence.

One sentence gives us settled being.

The other gives us happening.

This difference is not decoration around an otherwise identical meaning.

It contributes to how the reality is offered for inhabitation.

Now consider:

“She closed the door.”

Again, the physical outcome may be the same.

But agency has entered.

The listener is made to stand in relation not merely to a condition or occurrence, but to an act performed by someone.

The sentence has reorganised the field.

What survives is different.

From:

“The door is closed,”

a condition survives.

From:

“The door closed,”

an occurrence survives.

From:

“She closed the door,”

an action survives with an agent bound to it.

The proposition cannot be reduced to a neutral packet that receives three stylistic coverings.

The form of completion changes what becomes available for later inheritance.

A later sentence can now ask:

“Why is it closed?”

“What made it close?”

“Why did she close it?”

Each continuation inherits a different landing.

Three questions

The architecture I am proposing begins with three questions.

The first is:

What work did the sentence perform?

The second is:

How did attention move in order for that work to be completed?

The third is:

What kind of thing remained standing once the movement was complete?

I call these three dimensions:

Work.

Orientation.

Survivor.

Every completed sentence, I propose, can be examined through these three dimensions.

The coordinate produced by them does not tell us everything the sentence means.

It names the form of completion.

It is an address for the landing.

Work

Let us begin with Work.

A sentence may do one of six fundamental kinds of work.

It may distinguish.

It may expand.

It may relate.

It may correct.

It may integrate.

Or it may bring something to rest.

These terms are not rhetorical themes.

They identify the principal operation through which the sentence achieves completion.

Distinction

A sentence performs Distinction when it makes one thing stand apart from another.

“This is not the same problem.”

“Only one witness saw the event.”

“The red wire, not the blue one.”

A boundary becomes operative.

The field is no longer undifferentiated.

One possibility is separated from another.

Distinction is not limited to explicit contrast words such as “not,” “only” or “rather.”

A sentence may distinguish by identifying a single member, establishing a category, or drawing a boundary that was not previously governing attention.

The work is complete when the difference can stand.

Expansion

A sentence performs Expansion when it opens what is already present into further intelligible extent.

“The report includes evidence from three additional hospitals.”

“She was not merely disappointed; she felt betrayed.”

“The problem affects housing, transport and public health.”

The sentence gives more room to what had been too narrow.

Expansion does not merely add words.

A verbose sentence may fail to expand anything.

Expansion occurs when the intelligible field itself becomes larger.

A previously compressed object acquires further dimensions, consequences, instances or degrees.

The work is complete when the field can now bear more than it bore before.

Relation

A sentence performs Relation when it makes a connection stand.

“The delay was caused by a software failure.”

“Her decision changed the course of the investigation.”

“The second result depends upon the first.”

Two or more terms may already be available.

What was not yet standing was their relation.

Cause, dependence, sequence, ownership, resemblance, opposition, membership and location are all possible forms of relation.

The work is complete when the terms can no longer be encountered as merely adjacent.

They now bear upon one another.

Correction

A sentence performs Correction when it refuses or replaces an available landing.

“The meeting is on Thursday, not Wednesday.”

“He did not resign; he was dismissed.”

“That conclusion does not follow from the evidence.”

Correction is not merely the introduction of new information.

It is answerable to a prior path that must now be revised.

Something had already become available or was in the process of becoming available.

The sentence intervenes against it.

This is why correction carries a distinctive inheritance.

It preserves the refused possibility as part of its own structure.

To understand the correction, one must know what is being corrected.

Integration

A sentence performs Integration when previously distinct elements are brought into a larger intelligible whole.

“Taken together, these failures indicate a breakdown in oversight.”

“The fear, the secrecy and the sudden departure were all consequences of the same decision.”

“These findings form one pattern.”

The parts are not erased.

They remain distinguishable.

But they now stand within a unity that changes their significance.

Integration is not mere listing.

A list preserves plurality.

Integration makes the plurality answerable to a whole.

The work is complete when the elements can be seen together without becoming identical.

Rest

A sentence performs Rest when it brings an active field to settlement.

“That is the final decision.”

“The matter is closed.”

“We now know what happened.”

Rest is not simply stopping.

A speaker can stop without completing.

Nor is rest automatically justified.

A sentence may pressure attention into rest before the evidence has earned it.

This distinction between landing and earned landing is vital.

Political rhetoric, propaganda, advertising, institutional authority and interpersonal manipulation can all use the language of closure while incompatible possibilities remain alive.

A sentence may sound final.

It may be grammatically complete.

It may arrive with confidence.

It may nevertheless fail to provide a path worthy of the rest it demands.

A theory of completion must therefore be capable of identifying false closure.

Otherwise, it becomes a closure machine.

Its task is not to force every utterance into completion.

Its task is to distinguish lawful landing from its imitation.

The six works are not subjects

These six forms of Work do not correspond to six kinds of subject matter.

A sentence about a war may distinguish, expand, relate, correct, integrate or rest.

A sentence about a cup of tea may do the same.

The work does not tell us whether the sentence is profound, trivial, true, false, moral, immoral, beautiful or ugly.

It tells us what kind of change has occurred in the field of intelligibility.

That is why the architecture can remain stable while content varies without obvious limit.

The same work can be performed upon different realities.

The same reality can be completed through different works.

One topic, different work

Consider a single topic: a company’s falling revenue.

“The decline began in the European market.”

That sentence may distinguish the relevant location from others.

“The decline affected all four product divisions.”

That sentence expands the extent of the problem.

“The decline followed the change in pricing.”

That sentence establishes a relation.

“The decline did not begin in Europe; it began in Asia.”

That sentence corrects a prior landing.

“The regional losses, product cancellations and customer departures form one pattern.”

That sentence integrates several elements.

“The decline has now ended.”

That sentence brings the active field to rest.

The subject remains approximately constant.

The work changes.

This is why semantic similarity does not determine structural identity.

Orientation

Work alone is not enough.

Two sentences may perform the same work while moving attention differently.

We therefore require a second dimension:

Orientation.

I use three terms:

Toward.

Across.

Away.

These do not describe physical direction.

They describe the direction of intelligible movement relative to what is becoming established.

Toward

A sentence moves Toward when attention advances into, approaches or increasingly determines what will stand.

“The figure in the distance is a lighthouse.”

The sentence moves from an insufficiently determined object toward identification.

“The cause was a damaged valve.”

Attention moves toward the term that completes the explanation.

“She became certain that he had lied.”

The movement advances into a determination.

Toward has the character of arrival.

The sentence moves into what will survive.

Across

A sentence moves Across when attention traverses a field of terms, stages, parts or relations.

“The fault spread from the battery to the main control system.”

“The argument moves from perception through memory to judgment.”

“She opened the letter, read the first line and left the room.”

The landing is achieved through traversal.

The sentence does not simply approach one term.

It carries attention across an articulated region.

Across is central to sequence, comparison, distribution and mediated relation.

Away

A sentence moves Away when completion is achieved through withdrawal, exclusion, release, refusal or departure from an available term.

“This is not a criminal matter.”

“The evidence no longer supports that conclusion.”

“She abandoned the original plan.”

The movement gains determination by leaving something behind.

Away does not mean that nothing survives.

On the contrary, what is refused, excluded or departed from may remain structurally present as the condition from which the sentence moves.

A negation must preserve enough of what it rejects for the rejection to be intelligible.

Work and orientation are independent

The same Work can move in different orientations.

Take Correction.

Toward Correction:

“The correct date is Thursday.”

Attention moves into the replacement.

Across Correction:

“The date changed from Wednesday to Thursday after the venue became unavailable.”

Attention traverses the alteration.

Away Correction:

“It is not on Wednesday.”

Attention completes through departure from the refused term.

All three sentences perform corrective work.

But the listener does not travel through them in the same way.

Now take Relation.

Toward Relation:

“The missing files point to deliberate interference.”

Across Relation:

“The files passed from the local server through the external account.”

Away Relation:

“The evidence separates the contractor from the breach.”

Again, the work is relational.

The movement differs.

This second dimension prevents the system from collapsing every sentence with similar content into one class.

It preserves the route.

And the route matters because another mind must be able to arrive.

Survivor

We now come to the third dimension.

When the work has been performed and the movement has landed, what kind of thing remains available?

I call this the Survivor.

There are three fundamental survivors:

Is.

With.

Does.

These names are deliberately simple.

They do not refer only to the literal presence of the words “is,” “with,” or “does.”

They identify three ways in which a completed act can remain standing.

Is

An Is-survivor leaves a condition, identity, state or determinate presence.

“The room is empty.”

“The object is bronze.”

“The result remains uncertain.”

Something stands as being the case.

The sentence may have reached that condition through distinction, correction, expansion or any other Work.

What survives is a settled term of being.

With

A With-survivor leaves a relation, accompaniment, dependency, arrangement or structured coexistence.

“The key is with the caretaker.”

“The decision depends on the appeal.”

“The two failures belong to the same process.”

What survives cannot be adequately stated as an isolated object or action.

It is a configuration.

One term stands with, against, through, within or in dependence upon another.

Does

A Does-survivor leaves an act, occurrence, performance or operative change.

“The alarm sounded.”

“She rejected the offer.”

“The temperature fell.”

What survives is not merely a condition.

Something happened, was done, or became operative.

Again, this does not depend upon grammatical tense or the literal use of a verb of action.

A sentence may contain many verbs and still leave a condition as its survivor.

The question is not which words appear.

It is what remains standing after the sentence has completed.

Returning to the door

We can now return to our earlier examples.

“The door is closed.”

The sentence leaves a condition.

Its survivor is Is.

“The door closed.”

The sentence leaves an occurrence.

Its survivor is Does.

“She closed the door.”

Again, the survivor is Does, but now agency is internal to the occurrence.

“The door is closed with a steel bolt.”

Now the completion may leave a structured arrangement.

Its survivor is With.

The topic remains the door.

The underlying event may be closely related.

But the sentence gives another consciousness a different place to stand.

The coordinate

A completed sentence can now be described through three questions:

What Work was completed?

What Orientation carried attention through that work?

What Survivor remained?

Six Works.

Three Orientations.

Three Survivors.

Six multiplied by three multiplied by three gives fifty-four possible coordinates.

But I want to resist the temptation to display the entire table too soon.

A table can make a discovery look like a taxonomy.

It can give the impression that the project consists of assigning labels to sentences.

That is not the central claim.

The central claim is that the sentence is an event of lawful completion, and that the event has an analysable architecture.

The coordinate is not imposed upon the sentence from outside.

It is an attempt to name the invariant produced by the sentence’s movement.

The invariant is not the whole meaning.

It is the form of completion that remains stable enough to be recognised across changes in subject matter, vocabulary, speaker and language.

Neighbouring sentences

The architecture is best seen not through isolated examples, but through neighbouring sentences.

Consider:

“The child is safe.”

“The child reached safety.”

“The child is safe with her mother.”

All three concern a child and safety.

But they do not land in the same place.

“The child is safe” leaves a condition.

“The child reached safety” leaves an occurrence of arrival.

“The child is safe with her mother” leaves a relational arrangement.

Now consider:

“The child is safe.”

“The child is not in danger.”

“The child is safe again.”

These may be almost interchangeable in practical use.

Yet the path differs.

The first establishes a condition directly.

The second achieves completion by moving away from danger.

The third inherits a prior loss of safety and restores the condition.

Similar meaning.

Different movement.

Different inheritance.

Different landing.

A listener may act similarly upon all three.

But the route made available to attention is not the same.

Why the route matters

Why should any of this matter?

Because the route is part of what a later act inherits.

Suppose a physician says:

“The patient is stable.”

A condition has been installed.

Suppose instead:

“The patient has stabilised.”

An occurrence has been installed.

Suppose:

“The patient is stable with respiratory support.”

A relation has been installed.

Each sentence may be clinically relevant.

Each may be true.

But each licenses different continuations.

“How long has the patient been stable?”

“What caused the stabilisation?”

“Can the support be reduced?”

The future discourse is shaped by what the prior sentence left available.

This is what I mean by lawful remainder.

The sentence reaches rest, but the human world is not finished by understanding.

The landing becomes the starting condition of what may intelligibly follow.

A mathematical equation often seeks a settlement that minimises remainder within the operation.

A sentence reaches a rest that generates further lawful possibility.

It can be answered.

Challenged.

Remembered.

Applied.

Carried into another domain.

Used as evidence.

Used as a premise.

Used as a command.

Used as the beginning of a story.

Its completion is not death.

It is availability.

A public survivor of constraint

We can now state the object more precisely.

A completed sentence is a public survivor of constraint.

It is public not because everyone necessarily hears or accepts it, but because its completion can, in principle, be entered by another consciousness.

It is a survivor because the originating event can end while the completed result remains available.

And it is a survivor of constraint because it did not arrive arbitrarily.

Possibilities were selected.

Continuations were removed.

Relations were preserved.

A particular landing became capable of standing.

This is why a sentence can be reread.

Rereading is not merely repetition.

It is a test of whether another traversal can recover the same invariant.

Can another mind arrive here by the same path?

Can the completion survive a different voice?

A different page?

A different century?

A different language?

A different interpreter?

If the answer is yes, then something more than a private mental occurrence has taken place.

A shareable route has been stabilised.

Translation

This also changes how we might understand translation.

Translation is often described as the transfer of meaning from one language to another.

But “meaning” can conceal several different demands.

A translation may preserve reference while altering the movement.

It may preserve emotional force while altering the survivor.

It may preserve the proposition while changing the place from which the listener is made to view it.

If the architecture is correct, structural fidelity requires more than selecting equivalent words.

It requires asking whether the translated sentence performs the same Work, follows the same Orientation, and leaves the same kind of Survivor.

This does not mean that one exact form must always be preserved.

Languages possess different resources.

A translator may have to change grammar, word order, idiom or metaphor.

But beneath those changes, the landing may remain structurally conserved.

The original and the translation may arrive at the same coordinate by materially different paths.

That would give us a more exact account of what it means to say that a translation has preserved the act.

The media again

We can now return to the news and see more clearly what is at stake.

A report does not merely supply propositions.

It performs repeated structural operations upon public attention.

A headline distinguishes one event from the background.

A segment expands its apparent importance.

A commentator relates it to a larger crisis.

An expert corrects a rival interpretation.

A panel integrates several incidents into a trend.

A presenter brings the discussion to rest:

“What is now clear is that confidence has collapsed.”

Each completed sentence leaves a survivor.

A condition.

A relation.

An action.

These survivors are inherited by the next sentence.

After enough inheritance, a public reality takes shape.

This is not necessarily deception.

Any intelligible account must select.

Any explanation must relate.

Any report must exclude what it cannot include.

The ethical and epistemic question is whether the landing has been earned.

Has the evidence actually contracted the field?

Have incompatible continuations genuinely been removed?

Has the relation been established?

Or has the language of completion arrived before the path required to justify it?

This gives the system a critical use.

It can ask not only:

“What is this sentence claiming?”

but:

“What work is this sentence doing to attention?”

“How is it moving the listener?”

“What survivor is it attempting to install?”

“What possibilities has it removed?”

“And has it earned the rest it demands?”

A sentence can complete falsely

At this point, an objection naturally appears.

What about false sentences?

What about absurd sentences?

What about lies?

Can a sentence become complete if what it says is untrue?

Yes.

Truth and completion cannot be identical.

A lie may be structurally complete.

A false accusation can land.

A conspiracy theory can integrate unrelated events into a coherent whole.

Propaganda can bring attention to rest.

An absurd sentence may establish enough structure to demand interpretation.

This is precisely why completion matters.

A false sentence can still leave a survivor.

It can still condition later thought.

It can still become available for repetition, resistance or belief.

The distinction we need is not between completed and true.

It is among:

completion,

earned completion,

and truthful completion.

These may coincide.

But they need not.

A sentence may complete without earning its landing.

It may earn a limited landing while making an excessive claim.

It may be structurally coherent and factually false.

It may be factually accurate but structurally misleading.

The architecture does not relieve us of epistemology.

It gives epistemology another object to inspect.

The danger of premature classification

There is also a danger internal to this theory.

Once a finite architecture exists, the analyst may begin forcing every utterance into a coordinate.

That would reproduce the very error the theory is designed to expose.

Not every sequence of words completes.

Some utterances remain ambiguous.

Some are interrupted.

Some are internally incoherent.

Some leave multiple viable landings.

Some depend upon context we do not possess.

Some only appear complete because authority, repetition or expectation has pressured us into rest.

A lawful theory must preserve the right to refuse classification.

It must be able to say:

“This sentence has not yet earned a coordinate.”

Or:

“More than one reading remains alive.”

Or:

“The surface is closed, but the underlying movement is unresolved.”

A system that completes everything is not a completion system.

It is a closure machine.

The deeper proposal

The proposal can now be stated in fuller form.

Every genuinely completed sentence performs a determinate kind of Work.

It carries attention through that work with a determinate Orientation.

And it leaves a determinate kind of Survivor.

The combination forms the invariant of the landing.

That invariant does not replace the sentence’s meaning.

It does not exhaust its historical, emotional, political or literary significance.

It identifies the architecture through which the sentence became complete enough to bear.

If this is correct, then beneath the enormous diversity of language there exists a finite manifold of completed intelligibility.

Not a finite list of meanings.

Not a finite list of thoughts.

Not a finite list of sentences.

A finite architecture of landing.

What would follow

If this architecture is real, several consequences follow.

First, representation is downstream from completion.

Before something can stand for something else, it must become capable of standing.

Second, communication is not merely transfer.

It is the re-realisation of a completed route in another act of awareness.

Third, structure is not neutral packaging around meaning.

Structure helps determine how another consciousness is positioned in relation to what is said.

Fourth, public discourse is cumulative inheritance.

Each landing prepares the field into which later sentences enter.

Fifth, falsehood can be consequential because completion and truth are distinct.

Sixth, translation may preserve architecture across linguistic change.

Seventh, rereading becomes a test of recoverability.

And eighth, agency becomes more extensive than intention at the moment of speech.

A person may complete an act whose survivor enters minds, institutions and situations the speaker will never encounter.

The next question

But a serious theory cannot remain at the level of elegant description.

It must become testable.

We now have to ask:

Can independent readers identify the same Work, Orientation and Survivor?

Can neighbouring coordinates be distinguished reliably?

Can the same coordinate be recognised across unrelated subject matter?

Can translations preserve the invariant?

Can false or manipulative closure be distinguished from earned completion?

Can the path through a sentence be reconstructed rather than merely labelled after the fact?

And perhaps most importantly:

Are these invariants simply interpretive conveniences, or do they correspond to something that cognition itself must accomplish whenever intelligibility lands?

Those are empirical questions.

The architecture does not become true because it is finite, elegant or memorable.

It becomes defensible only if it explains observable regularities better than competing accounts and survives attempts to make it fail.

So the next movement is not a declaration of victory.

It is the transition from phenomenon to method.

If completed intelligibility has an architecture, how would we demonstrate it?

Movement 3 of 4

Final movement: From architecture to method

If completed intelligibility has an architecture, then the theory must eventually submit to more than intuition.

It must become possible to test whether the proposed coordinates correspond to stable features of sentence completion or merely to the interpretive habits of the person applying them.

This is the point at which the project must move from demonstration to accountability.

The question is no longer only:

“Can I show you examples that seem to fit?”

The question becomes:

“Can the system survive disagreement, independent classification, translation, adversarial examples and repeated rereading?”

That is the standard the proposal must meet.

What would count as evidence?

The first form of evidence would be recognitional stability.

Suppose several readers are given completed sentences without being told their subject matter is relevant.

Can they independently identify the same Work?

Can they agree upon the Orientation?

Can they recognise the kind of Survivor left by the sentence?

Perfect agreement would not be required.

Human interpretation rarely produces perfect agreement, even in established domains.

But the agreement would need to be substantially greater than chance and sufficiently stable to indicate that readers are responding to something in the sentence rather than inventing a structure after the fact.

The strongest tests would not use obvious examples.

They would use neighbouring coordinates.

The system should not merely distinguish a command from a description or a question from a statement.

Existing grammar can already do that.

It should distinguish sentences that are semantically close, grammatically similar and pragmatically interchangeable, yet complete through different movements.

For example:

“The patient is stable.”

“The patient has stabilised.”

“The patient remains stable with respiratory support.”

The content overlaps heavily.

The practical implications may overlap.

But the landing differs.

One leaves condition.

One leaves occurrence.

One leaves structured relation.

If independent readers can reliably detect such differences, the architecture begins to acquire empirical standing.

The importance of neighbouring cases

A weak taxonomy proves itself through distant examples.

A strong architecture proves itself at the borders.

It is easy to distinguish:

“The room is cold”

from:

“Please close the window.”

It is harder to distinguish sentences whose differences are slight enough that ordinary semantic analysis is tempted to treat them as equivalent.

That difficulty is useful.

The theory claims that structure contributes something definite even when overall meaning remains similar.

It therefore has to work precisely where semantic similarity is strongest.

Consider:

“The road is clear.”

“The road has cleared.”

“The debris was removed from the road.”

“The road is clear of debris.”

“The road is no longer blocked.”

Each may describe roughly the same practical situation.

A vehicle may now pass.

But each gives attention a different route.

Condition.

Occurrence.

Action.

Relation.

Departure from obstruction.

The system should be able to say not only that these sentences differ, but how they differ and what kind of remainder each leaves available.

Classification must include refusal

A valid method would also need a refusal condition.

Analysts must be able to withhold classification when the evidence is insufficient.

That may occur when a sentence is ambiguous.

It may occur when essential context is missing.

It may occur when the apparent landing depends entirely upon tone.

It may occur when the utterance has been interrupted.

It may occur when two structurally distinct readings remain genuinely alive.

The purpose of a finite system is not to force every string of words into one of fifty-four cells.

The claim concerns completed sentences.

So the method must distinguish between:

a completed sentence,

an apparently completed sentence,

a multiply completed sentence,

and a sequence that has not yet completed.

This is not an inconvenience at the edge of the theory.

It is one of the conditions of its seriousness.

A classifier that never refuses is not discovering invariants.

It is distributing labels.

Rereading as a test

Rereading becomes central here.

The first reading of a sentence may be dominated by subject matter, familiarity, emotional force or expectation.

On rereading, attention can be directed toward the path by which the sentence landed.

What became available first?

What remained open?

Where did the sentence narrow the field?

Which continuations became impossible?

What was preserved through the movement?

What exactly stood at the end?

A useful annotation method would require the analyst to provide a receipt for the classification.

Not merely:

“This sentence is Relation–Across–With.”

But:

“These are the terms initially available.”

“This is the relation established between them.”

“This is the traversal required to establish it.”

“These rival continuations were removed.”

“This structured coexistence is what remains.”

The coordinate would be the compressed result.

The receipt would expose the path.

Without the receipt, the system risks becoming impressionistic.

With the receipt, another reader can inspect the landing, challenge it, reconstruct it or refuse it.

Invariants as products of operations

This suggests that the fifty-four coordinates may not be the deepest level of the system.

They may be terminal addresses produced by ordered sequences of operations.

A sentence may distinguish before it relates.

It may expand before it integrates.

It may establish a possible interpretation before correcting it.

It may move toward a term, across a relation and away from an excluded alternative before reaching completion.

The final coordinate names the invariant of the landing.

But the invariant may be composed from a temporal history.

That possibility matters because sentences unfold.

The words do not appear simultaneously to the listener.

Attention is changed incrementally.

Expectations arise and are revised.

Possible endings become more or less likely.

A complete account may therefore need to distinguish between the final structural address and the path of operations that produced it.

The coordinate would answer:

“Where did the sentence land?”

The operational sequence would answer:

“How did it get there?”

This gives the theory a possible bridge to psycholinguistics and neuroscience.

If the sentence is processed over time, then the proposed operations may correspond to detectable changes in expectation, constraint, integration, revision or closure.

That is a research programme, not a result already obtained.

But the theory makes the programme conceivable.

A possible experimental design

Imagine an experiment in which participants encounter sentences word by word.

At selected points, they are asked which continuations still seem viable.

As the sentence unfolds, the field of possible continuation contracts.

Some possibilities disappear gradually.

Others collapse after a decisive word.

Some sentences invite one apparent landing and then correct it.

Some preserve several possible structures until the final phrase.

The experiment could compare these trajectories across sentences assigned to different coordinates.

Do sentences with the same final invariant produce recognisably similar patterns of constraint?

Do Toward sentences show increasing convergence upon a term?

Do Across sentences sustain and connect multiple active elements?

Do Away sentences preserve an excluded possibility long enough for departure from it to remain intelligible?

Do Is, With and Does survivors produce different patterns of continuation, memory or inference?

These questions could be investigated through behavioural measures, reading times, eye movements, recall tasks, prediction tasks and eventually neural measures.

The system does not require that one specific experimental method confirm it.

But it must generate discriminable predictions.

Translation as a stronger test

Translation offers another demanding test.

Suppose a sentence is translated into several languages.

The grammar may change.

Word order may change.

Some languages may require distinctions that others leave implicit.

A direct lexical equivalent may be impossible.

Yet skilled speakers may still judge the translation to preserve the same act.

What exactly has been preserved?

If Work, Orientation and Survivor remain stable across materially different linguistic forms, that would support the claim that the coordinate is not merely an artefact of English grammar.

The test would need to be conducted carefully.

One could not assume in advance that every good translation must preserve the coordinate.

Some translations deliberately alter emphasis, movement or relation in order to preserve tone, cultural intelligibility or practical effect.

That variation would itself be informative.

The method could distinguish:

semantic preservation,

pragmatic preservation,

stylistic preservation,

and structural preservation.

The point would not be to declare one of these the only legitimate form of fidelity.

It would be to identify what has changed and what has remained.

The sentence and the brain

The proposal also invites a neurological question, though caution is essential.

It would be premature to assign each Work, Orientation or Survivor to a particular brain region.

Language processing is distributed.

The neural systems involved in syntax, semantics, prediction, memory, action and social cognition interact dynamically.

The theory should not be reduced to a new phrenology in which one cell of the manifold is placed in one location of the cortex.

The more appropriate question is functional:

What must a cognitive system accomplish for an intelligible act to become complete?

It must preserve relevant terms.

It must exclude incompatible continuations.

It must establish relations.

It must detect correction.

It must integrate parts.

It must recognise when sufficient determination has been achieved for the act to stand.

If the proposed architecture is real, then these operations may be reflected not as isolated modules but as recurrent patterns of coordination across neural systems.

The scientific question would be whether different forms of landing produce distinguishable temporal signatures.

Does a corrective sentence produce a recognisable reorganisation of expectation?

Does integration require a different pattern from distinction?

Does a Does-survivor alter subsequent prediction differently from an Is-survivor?

Again, these are hypotheses.

The theory does not yet possess the evidence required to answer them.

But it specifies an object that could be investigated:

the completion of intelligibility as an event.

What would falsify the theory?

A theory becomes stronger when it states the conditions under which it would fail.

This proposal would be weakened if independent analysts could not classify sentences above chance.

It would be weakened if the same sentence moved freely among coordinates without any principled basis for choosing among them.

It would be weakened if neighbouring coordinates could not be distinguished except by reference to subject matter.

It would be weakened if translations systematically destroyed the proposed invariants even when competent speakers judged the acts to be preserved.

It would be weakened if the three dimensions proved reducible to ordinary grammatical categories without explanatory gain.

It would be weakened if the system classified only carefully selected examples and failed on spontaneous language.

It would be weakened if the full range of completed sentences required constant exceptions, auxiliary categories or post hoc reinterpretations.

And it would fail in its strongest form if genuinely completed sentences were shown to require additional fundamental dimensions not derivable from Work, Orientation and Survivor.

The number fifty-four must not be protected from evidence.

If the architecture is real, testing will refine it.

If it is incomplete, testing will expose the missing dimension.

If it is wrong, testing should make that visible.

The purpose of the method is not to preserve the theory.

It is to make the object answerable.

The claim is architectural, not imperial

This is also why the system should not present itself as a replacement for linguistics, philosophy, psychology or neuroscience.

Grammar remains necessary.

Semantics remains necessary.

Pragmatics remains necessary.

Logic remains necessary.

Historical and social interpretation remain necessary.

The architecture of completion would occupy another level of description.

Grammar may tell us how a sentence is formed.

Semantics may tell us what it denotes or entails.

Pragmatics may tell us what a speaker accomplishes in context.

The Method of Resolution asks:

How does this particular act become complete enough to stand, survive and enter what follows?

Those questions overlap, but they are not identical.

The proposal is strongest when it identifies a missing object rather than claiming jurisdiction over every neighbouring discipline.

Why has the object been difficult to see?

If sentence completion is so fundamental, why has it remained obscure?

One reason may be fluency.

Fluent understanding conceals its own construction.

The listener does not ordinarily experience the elimination of incompatible continuations.

The listener experiences the sentence as simply making sense.

The completed act arrives with its path compressed.

We see what stands.

We do not see everything that had to stop being possible for it to stand.

This is similar to many skilled activities.

A fluent reader does not consciously decode every letter.

A skilled musician does not calculate every movement.

A familiar face is recognised without awareness of every discriminating feature.

Success makes the supporting architecture transparent.

The sentence may therefore be overlooked not because it is rare, but because it is universal and fast.

It disappears into understanding.

The ethical dimension

There is also an ethical reason to study completion.

Human beings are vulnerable not only to false information but to unearned rest.

We can be brought to feel that a matter is settled before the relations have been established.

Authority can imitate evidence.

Repetition can imitate necessity.

Confidence can imitate completion.

Emotion can collapse alternatives before they have been examined.

Institutional language can make a decision appear to follow inevitably from conditions that were themselves constructed by the discourse.

The critical question is therefore not only:

“Is this sentence true?”

It is also:

“What kind of landing is being demanded of me?”

“What has been excluded?”

“What relation has been installed?”

“What survivor is being placed into my future thought?”

“And was the path worthy of the rest?”

This is not a call to resist every completion.

Without completion, shared reality would be impossible.

We could not teach, promise, decide, explain, remember or act together.

The ethical task is not permanent openness.

It is earned closure.

A sentence should be allowed to stand when its path can bear inspection.

Agency reconsidered

This returns us to agency.

We often treat agency in language as the speaker’s capacity to choose words and express intentions.

But the consequences of sentence completion extend beyond the speaker’s present purpose.

A sentence may leave a survivor that enters another person’s judgment.

That survivor may be repeated, resisted, translated, institutionalised or forgotten and later recovered.

The speaker may never witness those later acts.

The original event ends.

Its remainder continues to bear.

Agency in language therefore includes the capacity to establish a completion whose consequences exceed the moment of utterance.

But the listener is not merely passive.

The listener re-realises the act.

The listener may preserve the survivor, transform it, refuse it or expose that the landing was unearned.

Language is not a movement from an active speaker into a passive recipient.

It is a chain of constrained realisations.

Each act receives something.

Each act performs new work upon what it receives.

Each act leaves a remainder of its own.

Culture as inherited completion

From this perspective, culture can be understood as an immense field of inherited completions.

A law is a completed act that continues to structure future acts.

A scientific concept is a survivor that becomes available to researchers who did not establish it.

A story carries completed relations into minds separated by centuries.

A ritual stabilises a route that can be walked again.

An institution preserves decisions, distinctions and obligations beyond the lives of their authors.

Education installs survivors into new fields of attention.

Tradition is not merely repetition of the past.

It is the governed re-entry of completed acts into present awareness.

This does not guarantee fidelity.

Inheritance can distort.

Carriers can fail.

Routes can be shortened until their conclusions remain while their reasons disappear.

That is why records, proofs, citations, archives and methods matter.

They preserve not only what landed, but how the landing was earned.

We can now return one final time to Dickens.

The sentence survives because its words have been reproduced.

But that is not enough.

Random marks could also be reproduced.

What survives is a traversable completion.

The sentence establishes a contrast and holds the opposing conditions together.

It gives another consciousness a place to stand within simultaneous extremity.

A reader in 2026 does not recover Dickens’s original neural state.

The reader re-realises the lawful route.

The material body changes.

The route remains available.

The partner says:

“Oh, Dickens.”

Recognition occurs because the completion has not dissolved into the carrier.

It has remained sufficiently determinate to be inherited.

That is the phenomenon.

The proposal in one sentence

The entire proposal can now be stated in one sentence:

A completed sentence is a lawful contraction of possible attention into a public survivor of constraint, achieved through a determinate Work and Orientation and leaving a determinate form of remainder available for future acts.

Can we distinguish the survivor of a result from the survivor of the path by which that result was earned?

Can the inheritance of one completed act constrain the formation of the next?

And are Work, Orientation and Survivor all primitive, or might Orientation and Survivor emerge from deeper compositions of primitive Work?

These questions have not been left entirely untouched.

The project has already been pursued along several avenues.

The fifty-four coordinates have been compared pair by pair.

All 1,431 unique pairs remain distinguishable in the current written specification. Their distinctions do not depend upon their names. The names function as handles, but the complete movements do the differentiating work. This establishes internal discreteness within the present architecture, although it does not establish universal or empirical completeness.

The field has also been studied as a periodic structure rather than merely a list.

The nine lifecycle columns hold Orientation and Survivor constant while Work changes, making it possible to see how one topology and one persistence mode are transformed across Distinction, Expansion, Relation, Correction, Integration and Rest.

A multilingual recovery instrument has been constructed.

It preserves the source sentence, spreads it across ten languages, exhausts the meaning shared across those realisations, recovers the exact unresolved condition, the answering movement and the landing, then derives the coordinate and withholds judgment wherever positive recovery fails.

That method is designed to prevent the table from deciding the sentence in advance.

The sentence must be recovered before the coordinate is revealed.

The address is a verification target, not a construction premise.

The project has therefore begun to test not only whether the cells can be named, but whether they can be reached without circularity.

The deeper generative question has also been opened.

A primitive-Work chemistry asks whether the fifty-four are simply combinations of three independent dimensions or whether their final forms may arise from typed operations, ordered dependencies, repetition, reciprocity, nesting, distribution and other forms of lawful composition.

If that avenue succeeds, the table may prove to be not merely a geometry of completed acts, but the visible surface of a generative system beneath them.

Translation has been used not simply as an application but as a philosophical instrument.

The carrier may change radically while the completed act remains recoverable.

Sounds change.

Scripts change.

Grammar redistributes the work.

Historical and cultural conditions change.

Yet the event may still arrive in the same structural place.

The concentrated proposition is:

Change the carrier. Preserve the address.

This has consequences for how we think about memory, teaching, proof, law, quotation, literature and civilisation.

A completed act need not continue occurring in order to continue bearing.

The event ends.

A survivor remains.

That survivor may include not only the result, but the path through which the result was earned.

A proof survives differently from a slogan because the route remains available for another mind to walk.

A lawful inheritance does not merely repeat the conclusion.

It preserves enough of the construction for the conclusion to remain answerable.

This is why the project has also developed a principle of withholding.

Where context, meaning, movement, landing, Orientation or Survivor cannot be positively recovered, the coordinate must not be supplied.

Uncertainty must not be converted into classification merely because the table has an empty place waiting to be filled.

That principle applies to the theory itself.

The existence of fifty-four internally discrete specifications does not yet prove that every completed sentence occupies exactly one of them.

It does not prove that the field is exhaustive.

It does not prove that the architecture is psychologically natural.

It does not prove that independent analysts will agree.

And it does not prove that the same structures will be found in every language, domain or form of consciousness.

Those are not small qualifications attached to the edge of the theory.

They describe the next stage of the work.

The architecture now requires contact.

Not only with examples selected because they display the structure clearly, but with ordinary, resistant, ambiguous and unfamiliar language.

It requires reproduction.

Other readers must attempt the recovery without inheriting the discoverer’s expectations.

And it requires inheritance.

The survivor of each completed act must be followed into what comes next, until the table can be studied not only as a catalogue of landings but as a possible transition system.

Those are the three immediate demands now facing the project: contact, reproduction and inheritance.

So I do not offer this as a finished theory of language, consciousness or representation.

Nor do I offer it as an intuition that has not yet been worked upon.

It occupies a more interesting position.

A phenomenon has been isolated.

A vocabulary has been developed.

A finite architecture has been specified.

Its coordinates have been tested for internal discreteness.

A positive recovery protocol has been built.

A multilingual instrument has been used.

A withholding rule has been made binding.

A periodic structure has been examined.

And a deeper generative chemistry has begun to ask whether the visible dimensions themselves can be derived.

The work is therefore neither untouched nor complete.

It has travelled far enough to become answerable.

That may be the most important threshold.

A private recognition can be compelling without being shareable.

A theory becomes public only when another person can enter it, test its route, expose its weaknesses, preserve what survives and carry the inquiry somewhere its originator could not have anticipated.

The proposal I leave with you is therefore not merely that sentences have a hidden structure.

It is that completed intelligibility may itself have a finite and recoverable architecture.

That architecture may survive translation.

It may guide inheritance.

It may help distinguish earned completion from counterfeit closure.

It may expose how one act of awareness becomes portable enough to enter another.

And it may provide a new point of departure for questions that representation alone begins too late to answer.

Before something can represent, it must become capable of standing.

Before it can be transmitted, something must survive.

Before it can be inherited, a path must remain recoverable.

And before a shared world can continue, one completed act must leave another act somewhere lawful to begin.

That is where the work presently stands.

Not at the end of the inquiry.

At the point where the tracks beneath language have become visible enough for others to follow.
